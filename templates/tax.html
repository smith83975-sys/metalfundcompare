How gold & silver funds are taxed in the US

General information about US federal rules for individual investors, not tax advice. Rules change and your situation matters — check the fund's prospectus and tax documents, and talk to a tax professional.

Two funds can hold the same metal and still be taxed very differently. The structure decides it.

1. Physically backed trusts (GLD, IAU, SLV, SIVR, PPLT…): "collectibles"

Most US physical metal ETFs are grantor trusts. For tax purposes you are treated as owning a share of the metal itself, and the IRS treats precious metals as collectibles. Long-term gains (held more than one year) are taxed at your ordinary rate but capped at 28% — higher than the 15%/20% rates most stock gains get. Short-term gains are taxed as ordinary income. You get a regular 1099. Source: IRS Topic 409.

Because the trust sells small amounts of metal to pay its fee, holders can also have tiny taxable gains or losses each year; the trust's annual tax information explains this.

2. Sprott physical trusts (PHYS, PSLV, CEF, SPPP): PFIC + QEF election

These are Canadian trusts, so for US investors they are generally passive foreign investment companies (PFICs). Sprott publishes an annual PFIC statement so holders can make a Qualified Electing Fund (QEF) election on IRS Form 8621, filed every year. According to Sprott, with a timely QEF election, long-term gains may qualify for regular capital-gains rates instead of the 28% collectibles rate. Without an election, the harsher PFIC "excess distribution" rules can apply. Check Sprott's US tax materials and a professional — the paperwork is the trade-off.

3. Futures funds and leveraged commodity pools (DBP, UGL, AGQ…): 60/40 and K-1

Funds organized as commodity pools (partnerships) hold futures. Section 1256 futures are marked to market every year: gains count as 60% long-term / 40% short-term, whether or not you sold (IRS Form 6781). You get a Schedule K-1, not a 1099, which can arrive late in tax season.

4. Miners ETFs and other 1940-Act funds (GDX, GDXJ, SIL, RING…): regular stock-fund rules

These own shares of mining companies, so they are taxed like ordinary stock ETFs: long-term gains at 0/15/20%, dividends possibly qualified, and a 1099. Hybrid funds that hold stocks or futures through a regular fund structure (e.g. SLVR, GDE) also issue 1099s — check each prospectus.

5. Option-income funds (covered calls, "WeeklyPay", YieldBOOST…)

Distributions can be a mix of ordinary income, capital gains and return of capital. A high payout is not the same as a high total return. You get a 1099; the breakdown is published after year-end.

6. Leveraged / inverse ETFs (1940-Act)

Usually 1099 funds; distributions, when any, are often ordinary income or short-term gains. Because returns reset daily, long holding periods can produce results very different from 2× or −2× the metal.

7. ETNs (GDXU, GLDI, SLVO…)

An ETN is an unsecured note from a bank — you carry the bank's credit risk. Gains on sale are generally treated as capital gains, but the tax treatment of some ETNs is not settled. ETNs paying coupons usually generate ordinary income.

8. Canadian-listed funds (CGL, SVR, XGD…)

Listed on the TSX only. Canadian tax rules apply to Canadian investors; US investors may face PFIC rules. Included for completeness — data not yet verified.
